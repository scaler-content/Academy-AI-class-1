Section 1 · 7 minWhy AI is suddenly everywhere, and what you ship by Class 3

AI is about seventy years old. So why did it fill your feed only recently?

Two things changed, and between them they are the reason this class exists.

The model became general-purpose

Old AI did one task. A spam filter filtered spam and could do nothing else. Today's models write, summarise, translate, answer, and code, from one model. One engine, many uses.

The barrier collapsed

You do not build the model. The labs spend hundreds of millions doing that, and you call the result over the internet in a few lines of code.

Something that needed a team and six months a few years ago is now an afternoon for one person. That collapse in cost is the whole opportunity, and it is why this room exists.

What you are building

ImportantAcross these first three classes you build and ship one real thing: a web app that takes any URL, reads the page, and gives you a clean summary. Today you make your first call in code. Class 2 is the prompt that makes the summary good. Class 3 is the full app, the safety work, and a public link you can send to anyone.

Today is not the theory class before the real classes. Today is the first third of the build, with the concepts you need to not be lost in it.

Section 2 · 8 minFive words you will hear in every class

Five terms get used interchangeably in headlines and job posts. They are not the same thing.

Term What it is The test
AI The umbrella. Any software doing something we would call intelligent Your bank's fraud check is AI and writes nothing
Generative AI The slice that creates new content rather than scoring existing content It produces a thing that did not exist
LLM A generative model for text. The engine inside ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini You give it text, it continues the text
AI Agent An LLM given tools, memory and the ability to choose its next step It completes a task instead of answering a question
Agentic AI The paradigm. An agent is a thing you build inside it One is a philosophy, the other is a system you can point at

Ask a chatbot to book a flight and it gives you three websites. Ask an agent and it searches, compares, fills the form and reports back.

Important — An LLM is the engine, not the product. ChatGPT is a product. GPT is the model inside it. When a price list or a benchmark names GPT or Claude or Gemini, it is talking about the engine, which is the thing you will call in code today.

Doubt by LearnerIs a chatbot with a web search button an agent?

Partly. It uses a tool, which is one ingredient. What it does not do is choose its own sequence of steps. One tool call that you triggered is a feature. The test is who decides what happens next.

Section 3 · 14 minWhat an LLM does, and what a token is

The autocomplete that read everything

Your phone predicts your next word as you text. Now give that autocomplete most of the public internet, and let it predict not one word but thousands in a row.

That is an LLM. It is the world's most well-read autocomplete.

This sounds reductive and it is the most useful thing you will learn today. It explains why prompting works, because you are steering the prediction. It explains why models invent things, because a plausible next word is not the same as a true one. It explains why context matters, because the prediction is conditioned entirely on what is in front of it.

Tokens are the unit it reads

The model does not read words. It reads tokens, chunks of text that average around four characters in English.

Write "I love programming" and you see three words. The model sees more, because the long word gets chopped into pieces of its own.

Tokens matter for one very practical reason: tokens are the billing unit and tokens are the limit. You will see this on your own API bill in Section 10.

Why tokens, and not words or letters

The model needs a fixed vocabulary, a list of things it knows. Both obvious choices fail.

One token per word means millions of entries across every language, plus names, slang and typos, and it breaks the first time it meets a word it has never seen.

One token per letter means a tiny vocabulary but enormous sequences, which is slow and expensive, and the model has to rebuild spelling from scratch every time.

Sub-word tokens sit in between. Around a hundred thousand common chunks. Frequent words stay whole, anything rare is assembled from pieces. One fixed vocabulary handles every word in every language, including ones invented tomorrow.

Step 1 to Step 3: see your own tokens

Doubt by LearnerIf I write shorter prompts, do I always pay less?

Less on input, yes. Output tokens usually cost two to four times more than input tokens, so a vague short prompt that triggers a long rambling answer can cost more than a precise longer one. You pay for both halves.

Section 4 · 8 minThe trick that made all of this possible

This is the part that makes people sit up, and it answers a question you should be asking: how do you teach a machine language without an army of people labelling billions of examples?

The old way

Humans label data by hand. This email is spam. This review is positive. Accurate, and you need millions of labelled examples, so it is slow and expensive.

The shortcut

Take any sentence from the web. Hide a word. Ask the model to guess it.

The answer is already sitting in the text, so no human has to label anything. The internet becomes its own teacher, and you get trillions of free practice questions.

Note for InstructorDo this with the room before you explain it. Put the sentence on screen and ask them to call out the missing word: "The barista handed me a hot cup of ______." Let them answer, then make the point below. The order matters, because the point only lands once they have done it themselves.

To guess "coffee", you had to quietly know grammar, what a barista is, what is served hot, what fits in a cup, and what usually follows that phrasing. None of that was taught directly. It came out of getting very good at filling in blanks.

ImportantThat is the whole secret. Understanding emerges as a side effect of predicting text well. It also explains the failure mode: a model trained to produce plausible text will produce plausible text when it does not know, which is what people call hallucination. Class 3 is largely about designing around that.

Section 5 · 7 minNo memory, and the context window

Here is a fact that quietly explains a great deal of what you are about to build.

Between calls, the model remembers nothing. Nothing at all. Every chat application you have used creates the illusion of memory by re-sending the conversation every single time.

That has a limit, and the limit is the context window.

Picture a desk. Everything the model can see has to fit on it: your instructions, the conversation so far, anything you pasted, and the answer it is about to write. A small desk holds a few pages. A large one holds a codebase.

When you run out, papers fall off the edge, and the model genuinely loses what was at the start.

Ask LearnersWho has had a long chat where the model started contradicting something it said twenty messages earlier? That is the desk overflowing.
ImportantA large window is not the same as using it well. Accuracy drops in the middle of a very long context on every model. If something matters, put it near the start or the end, not buried in the middle of a long document.
Note for InstructorAdvanced learner extension, use only if the room is ahead: this is exactly why retrieval exists, and why today's app will send the page text on every single call rather than once. Point forward and move on.

Section 6 · 8 minThe dials: temperature, top-p, top-k

The model scores every possible next token. These three settings decide how it picks from that list.

Temperature controls how willing it is to pick something other than the most likely token. At 0 it takes the top candidate nearly every time, so the same input gives the same output. At 1 it reaches further down the list, which buys variety and occasionally nonsense.

Top-k caps the candidate list by count. Top-k of 5 means only the five most likely tokens are considered.

Top-p caps it by probability mass. Top-p of 0.9 keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to 0.9, which might be two tokens on a confident prediction and forty on an uncertain one. Top-p adapts to how sure the model is. Top-k does not. That is the difference worth remembering.

Step 1 and Step 2: watch the probabilities move

Note for InstructorLive demo, 4 minutes, using the visualizer rather than a chat window. A chat at 0.3 against 0.7 on a factual task looks nearly identical on camera and the point is lost.
1
Open https://andreban.github.io/temperature-topk-visualizer/ and drag temperature from 0 to 1. The bars flatten. Say what that means out loud: more candidates become plausible, so output varies between runs.
2
Set top-k to 1 and drag temperature again. Nothing changes. That is the lesson: top-k of 1 overrides temperature entirely, because there is only ever one candidate.
ImportantThis is a real engineering choice and you will make it in Section 10. A support bot wants about 0.2 so answers are consistent. A brainstorming tool wants about 1.0 because variety is the point. Today's summariser wants to be low.

Section 7 · 14 minThe landscape: four families, and the swappable brain

Specific model names go stale in weeks. Any list of the best model you memorise today will be wrong before this module ends. So learn the structure, which does not change, and where to look up today's names.

The four families

Family Who makes it Known for
GPT OpenAI Broad capability, the largest ecosystem
Claude Anthropic Instruction following, long documents, code review
Gemini Google DeepMind Very large context, multimodal including video
Open weights Meta, Mistral, DeepSeek, Alibaba and others You can download it and run it yourself

Fast models and reasoning models

This is the most important split in the landscape right now.

A fast model answers in one pass. It reads your prompt and starts writing immediately.

A reasoning model spends output tokens on itself first, working through the problem before it answers. More tokens, more seconds, better results on anything with several dependent steps.

Fast model Reasoning model
Latency Under a second to a few Several seconds to minutes
Cost Low Several times higher, thinking tokens are billed
Good at Classification, extraction, formatting, summarising Multi-step maths, planning, debugging

The split used to be a choice between two different models. On the newer open-weight models it is increasingly a dial on one model: you send a reasoning effort of low, medium or high and pay for the thinking you asked for.

The model you will use today, gpt-oss, works exactly that way. Summarising a page has no dependent steps, so you keep the effort low and it behaves like a fast model. That is not a budget compromise, it is matching the thinking to the job. You will set that dial in Class 2, where the app needs it; today's call stays minimal on purpose.

The brain is swappable

Here is the thing that makes all of this practical, and you will see it with your own eyes in Section 10.

Changing which company's model your app runs on is a two-line change. Same library, same code, same message format. You point the client at a different address and name a different model.

ImportantThat is the real reason to learn the structure rather than the names. You are not marrying a vendor. If a model gets better, cheaper, or is retired, you move, and your app does not care.

Section 9 · 8 minSetup: your key and your .env file

Note for InstructorHands-on, 8 minutes. Everyone does this at the same time. Editor and Python were pre-class homework; this section is only the key and the file.

An API key is a password that lets your code use somebody's model. We are using Groq, which gives one away free, with no billing details, and serves open-weight models very fast.

1
Go to https://console.groq.com and sign in. Open API Keys and create a key. Copy it immediately, because the site shows it once.
2
Make a folder for the project. Inside it, create a file named exactly .env, with the leading dot. Put your key in it.

The .env file holds your secret so it never appears in the code you share. That is the only thing it does.

# .env  --  this is not Python. It is a plain text file named .env
# Keep it private. Add it to .gitignore so it never reaches GitHub.

GROQ_API_KEY=gsk_your_key_goes_here
3
Install the three packages you need today.
# Run this once, in your editor's terminal, without the leading !
# In a notebook, keep the ! so it runs as a shell command.

!pip install openai python-dotenv
ImportantNever paste a key into code you share, and never commit one to GitHub. Bots scan public repositories for keys within minutes, and a leaked key spends real money. The key lives in .env, and .env goes in .gitignore. This is the one habit from today that genuinely saves people.
Doubt by LearnerWhy is a file called .env invisible in my folder?

Files starting with a dot are hidden by default on Mac and Linux. Your editor shows it. In Finder, press Command, Shift and full stop to reveal hidden files.

Note for InstructorThe most common failure here is a key pasted with a trailing space, or the file saved as .env.txt. Both produce the same confusing error. Check those two things first when someone is stuck.

Section 10 · 14 minYour first call: six lines and three roles

Note for InstructorHands-on, 14 minutes, and this is where the class lands. Everyone runs this and sees a reply. Do not move on until most of the room has output on screen.

What this code does

Four things, in order. It loads your key from .env. It creates a client, which is the object that knows how to talk to the model. It sends a list of messages. It prints the reply out of the response.

That is the whole shape of every AI application you will ever write, including the one in Class 3.

# first_call.py
# Loads the key, creates a client, sends messages, prints the reply.

import os
from dotenv import load_dotenv
from openai import OpenAI

load_dotenv()                    # reads .env so the key is available

key = os.getenv("GROQ_API_KEY")
if not key:
    raise SystemExit(
        "No GROQ_API_KEY found.\n"
        "Check three things, in this order:\n"
        "  1. The file is named exactly .env, not .env.txt\n"
        "  2. It sits in the same folder as this file\n"
        "  3. The line reads GROQ_API_KEY=gsk_... with no spaces around the ="
    )

# The same library talks to Groq. Only these two lines name a provider.
client = OpenAI(
    api_key=key,
    base_url="https://api.groq.com/openai/v1",
)

response = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="openai/gpt-oss-120b",
    temperature=0.2,             # low, because we want consistency
    messages=[
        {"role": "system", "content": "You are a witty travel guide."},
        {"role": "user",   "content": "Suggest one thing to do in Bangalore."},
    ],
)

print(response.choices[0].message.content)
Ask LearnersWho has a reply on screen? Put a yes in the chat.

The three roles

That messages list is the entire grammar of chat models. There are only three roles.

Role Who it is What it does
system You, the builder Sets the personality and the rules. Applies throughout
user The person using your app The actual request
assistant The model Its reply. To continue a conversation, you append it and resend the whole list

That last row is Section 5 made concrete. The model has no memory, so a conversation is you re-sending the entire list every time. Nothing else is happening.

Note for InstructorLive demo, 3 minutes. Change the system line to something silly, like a pirate or a tax inspector, and re-run. Same code, same model, different product. That is the Class 2 hook, so say it: next class is entirely about writing that one line well.

The swappable brain, proved

Change two lines and the same code runs on a different company's model.

# Same file, same messages, different provider.
# To use OpenAI instead of Groq, change only these two things:

client = OpenAI(
    api_key=os.getenv("OPENAI_API_KEY"),   # a different key in .env
    # no base_url line at all -- OpenAI is the library's default
)

# ...and name one of their models instead:
#     model="gpt-4o-mini"
ImportantNothing else moves. Same library, same message list, same way of reading the reply. This is why Section 7 taught families rather than names. OpenAI needs billing credit, so stay on Groq today unless you already have it.

Section 11 · 4 minWrap-up and what is next

What you can do now

You can explain what the model is doing when you press enter: predicting the next token from whatever is on its desk.

You can say why it can do that at all, which is self-supervision, and why that same trick produces confident wrong answers.

You can reason about tokens and context as the two hard limits, and as the bill.

You can pick a model deliberately: the family for its character, fast or reasoning for the shape of the task.

And you have made a real API call, with a key you own, from code on your machine.
